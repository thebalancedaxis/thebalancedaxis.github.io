PCOS manifests differently across ethnicities — metabolic dysfunction is more prominent in East Asian women, while inflammatory pathways dominate in European populations.

Know your phenotype

PCOS is not one condition. It's four.

The Rotterdam criteria (2003) define PCOS by three features: hyperandrogenism, chronic anovulation, and polycystic ovarian morphology. You need any 2 of 3 — which creates four distinct phenotypes with very different metabolic risks.

Rotterdam Diagnostic Criteria — diagnosis requires ≥ 2 of the following 3 features, after excluding other causes:

① Hyperandrogenism (HA) — elevated androgens (testosterone, DHEAS) or clinical signs (hirsutism, acne, alopecia)  ·  ② Ovulatory dysfunction (OD) — irregular or absent periods, chronic anovulation  ·  ③ Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) — ≥ 12 follicles per ovary or ovarian volume > 10 mL on ultrasound

Phenotype A · Classic

Full Classic PCOS

Hyperandrogenism
Ovulatory Dysfunction
Polycystic Ovaries
Highest metabolic risk

All three features present. Strongest association with insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, and cardiovascular risk.

Phenotype B · Classic

Classic without PCO

Hyperandrogenism
Ovulatory Dysfunction
Polycystic Ovaries
High metabolic risk

Similar hormonal profile to Phenotype A. Metabolic risk nearly as high — ovarian morphology alone doesn't define severity.

Phenotype C · Ovulatory

Ovulatory PCOS

Hyperandrogenism
Ovulatory Dysfunction
Polycystic Ovaries
Moderate metabolic risk

Regular cycles but elevated androgens and PCO morphology. Often under-recognised — women may not suspect PCOS at all.

Phenotype D · Non-androgenic

Mild PCOS

Hyperandrogenism
Ovulatory Dysfunction
Polycystic Ovaries
Lower metabolic risk

No androgen excess. Mildest metabolic profile. Some researchers debate whether this constitutes true PCOS.
